# P02 — Football Against the Odds

Courage. Safety. A place to play.

## 1. What survives when a match cannot happen?

A football team is usually described through its fixtures. It has a ground, opponents and a next match. Disruption can remove all three. What remains then? A shared name, friendships, memories of training and the determination to meet again may still connect the players. These connections matter, but they do not make danger smaller or replace a safe home. This story follows different ways people have protected a place for football when ordinary routines became difficult or impossible.

The histories must remain distinct. Evacuation from Afghanistan, building a club in Sarajevo and creating access to amputee football in Sierra Leone are not versions of one identical experience. Their connection is a question: what must other people help provide before a player can use her ability? Answering it takes us beyond the familiar idea that courage alone wins every contest.

## 2. A victory measured in safe passage

On 24 August 2021, FIFPRO thanked the Australian government for evacuating women footballers and other athletes from Afghanistan. Its statement recognised former captain Khalida Popal and a wider network of people working to secure safe passage. It also said that settlement and further support remained necessary. The immediate achievement was reaching safety; it was not a tournament result.

Notice how this changes the meaning of preparation. Football preparation normally concerns opponents, fitness or technique. In a crisis, people may instead need documents, reliable communication and decisions by authorities. A player's determination cannot independently supply those things. The football community mattered because people used relationships and expertise to act. Admiring the women is appropriate, but an account that mentions only bravery would hide the practical work that helped make departure possible.

## 3. Arrival is not the final whistle

UNHCR subsequently reported on the Afghan women's team playing in Australia. That later account gives us a different scene from the urgent statement about evacuation: teammates gathering for football in a new country. The contrast matters. Safety can allow sporting routines to become possible again, while resettlement still involves unfamiliar surroundings and difficult changes.

We should not invent what every player felt. An interview with one woman cannot tell us the private experience of an entire squad. Nor should a team photograph be treated as proof that every problem has disappeared. It establishes a moment of participation. A careful reader can recognise the importance of that moment without deciding that football has completed the work of rebuilding a life. People can enjoy a match and still need help away from it.

## 4. A club begins with noticing

In UEFA's account of SFK 2000 Sarajevo, founder Samira Hurem recalls seeing girls playing in parks and wondering why they had nowhere to train. The club began in 2000. This date matters: its foundation belongs after the Bosnian war, rather than being evidence that this particular club played throughout the siege of Sarajevo. Historical atmosphere must never replace chronology.

Hurem's starting point was a gap between interest and opportunity. Girls were already playing; a stable setting was missing. This challenges a common assumption about development. The first task is not always persuading people to want the game. Sometimes the desire already exists and the task is organising somewhere it can grow. Noticing unused potential becomes useful when someone turns that observation into regular activity, equipment and relationships that players can trust.

## 5. Rebuilding means repeating

A club is not built only on its opening day. It must arrange the next training session and the one after that. Sarajevo's history invites us to think about continuity as an achievement. A dramatic beginning attracts attention, but repeated work gives players the time needed to learn together. A dependable routine can be valuable precisely because it stops being dramatic.

Imagine a player learning to receive a pass under pressure. One inspiring afternoon gives her an introduction. Several months of suitable practice let her try, make mistakes, ask questions and improve. If sessions repeatedly disappear, that learning becomes harder. This example is an interpretation of why continuity matters, not a claim about a named Sarajevo player's training. Keeping that distinction clear lets us draw useful lessons without adding fictional incidents to the historical record.

## 6. Equipment is part of opportunity

The Salone Flying Stars in Sierra Leone describe an amputee football organisation with a women's team. Their website identifies practical needs including crutches, replacement rubber tips and transport. These details bring access into focus. An invitation is meaningful, but a person may still need usable equipment and a way to reach the session.

It would be a mistake to assume the same personal history for every member. An organisation's connection to a country affected by civil war does not establish how each individual acquired a disability. Likewise, equipment should not replace the footballer as the subject of the story. Players make decisions, develop skills and contribute to a team. Respect includes seeing those sporting qualities while also understanding the material conditions that allow them to be expressed.

## 7. Three kinds of support

Compare the examples carefully. Emergency assistance can protect immediate safety. Club organisation can create reliable opportunities over time. Suitable equipment and transport can make those opportunities usable. These forms of support can overlap, but none automatically supplies the others. A donated ball cannot substitute for safe passage; an evacuation cannot guarantee a permanent training ground.

This gives us a better way to evaluate a helpful action. Start with the barrier the player actually faces, then ask whether the proposed support addresses it. A well-meant gesture may still miss the need. Listening matters because the person receiving help understands parts of her situation that an outside observer cannot see. The strongest response combines practical knowledge with respect for the player's own priorities, rather than deciding those priorities on her behalf.

## 8. The problem with a perfect ending

Stories about hardship often move quickly towards a reassuring conclusion. A match is played, someone smiles and the audience feels that the struggle is finished. Real histories rarely offer such a tidy ending. A return to football can be a major achievement while uncertainty remains. A club can become successful and still need resources. These statements can both be true.

Avoiding a perfect ending does not make the story hopeless. It makes the achievement more precise. We can celebrate what people accomplished without claiming they solved everything. We can also recognise the responsibility of institutions, supporters and organisers. If courage is presented as the entire explanation, those with power may disappear from the account. If support is presented as the entire explanation, the players' own decisions disappear instead. Both belong in a responsible history.

## 9. Applying the lesson close to home

Your local football difficulties may be very different from these histories. A cancelled lift or a nervous first session should not be equated with forced displacement. Comparison is useful when it identifies a principle without pretending that circumstances are equal. Here the principle is that participation depends on conditions as well as enthusiasm.

Suppose a teammate rarely arrives on time. You could assume she cares less, or you could leave room for a practical explanation. An organiser might discover a transport problem and explore an arrangement with her family. The relevant response begins by finding out, rather than publicly judging her. This imagined example shows how a broad lesson about access can guide ordinary behaviour. It does not turn an everyday inconvenience into a historical crisis.

## 10. What against the odds should mean

The title celebrates persistence, but persistence does not require accepting danger or refusing help. Sometimes the strongest decision is leaving an unsafe place. Sometimes it is asking for support, resting or rebuilding at a manageable pace. A player should not have to perform an inspiring story for other people before her needs are taken seriously.

Remember the three movements of this card: protecting people, rebuilding routines and making participation usable. Each asks something of a wider community. The final test is not whether spectators find the account moving. It is whether we understand the barriers accurately and recognise the work that helped address them. Football can preserve relationships and create fresh possibilities. It does that best when the people in the story remain people with choices, rather than symbols whose only purpose is to inspire an audience.

# P03 — The Right to Play

Open the gate. Keep it open.

## 1. A gate is a decision

A football ground has a visible boundary, but access begins with decisions that may be less visible. Somebody decides who can book the pitch, which fixtures receive support and whose football is treated as worth organising. Those decisions can encourage a game or make it difficult to sustain. The right to play therefore involves more than a statement that anyone is welcome.

This card follows a distinction through women's football history: permission, recognition and practical opportunity are connected, but different. Permission means an activity is allowed. Recognition concerns its place within an organisation or official record. Practical opportunity means people can actually take part. Winning one can help secure the others, yet none should be assumed automatically. Keeping those categories separate makes the past clearer and helps us ask better questions about the present.

## 2. What the 1921 restriction did

On 5 December 1921, the English Football Association restricted women's matches on grounds belonging to affiliated clubs. The decision affected access to an important network of playing places. Describing it accurately matters. It was not a criminal law making every instance of women playing football illegal everywhere. Its consequences could still be profound without that exaggerated description.

Consider how a ground connects many parts of the game. It provides a surface, a place for spectators and a location around which a fixture can be organised. Losing access can disrupt those relationships together. A restriction need not confiscate a ball to make football harder. It can remove the ordinary arrangements that let a team return regularly. Understanding the mechanism helps us see why administrative decisions belong in sporting history alongside famous matches.

## 3. Surviving outside the centre

Women's football continued despite exclusion from that network. Manchester Corinthians, founded in 1949, became one example of a team creating opportunities through matches and travel. The FA's heritage account includes their international activity and charitable work. Their existence challenges the idea that the years of restriction were simply empty years without women playing.

At the same time, survival should not be used to excuse the restriction. A team finding alternatives does not prove that access was fair. People may achieve remarkable things under unequal conditions while still losing opportunities they ought to have had. Both observations belong together: credit the organisers and players for what they created, and examine the obstacles that made their work necessary. Otherwise resilience can accidentally become an argument for leaving a barrier in place.

## 4. Organisation becomes a form of change

The Women's Football Association formed in 1969. That development directs attention towards people who built structures, arranged competitions and represented the game. The historical work of an organiser may leave fewer dramatic photographs than a winning goal, but it can change the conditions in which many goals become possible.

Imagine trying to keep a competition running without a reliable fixture list or agreed arrangements. Individual skill cannot solve every disagreement about dates, venues and responsibilities. Administration is sometimes dismissed as separate from real football, yet it creates a framework for playing. The distinction worth making is between rules that support participation and decisions that unnecessarily exclude it. The existence of paperwork tells us little by itself; its purpose and effects are what need to be examined.

## 5. More than one date

The FA's own heritage history records its Council voting to rescind the 1921 resolution on 19 January 1970. It then describes deliberation and a relevant rule amendment at the Annual General Meeting on 24 June 1971. Recognition of the WFA followed in 1972. These dates identify stages in a process rather than three interchangeable accounts of one instant.

This is why a short slogan about the ban ending can conceal useful detail. A decision may be announced before all the arrangements needed to implement it have changed. Conversely, an institution's later recognition does not mean nobody organised football before that moment. A careful timeline asks what each event actually altered. Accuracy gives the people doing the work their proper place and avoids suggesting that progress arrived through a single effortless gesture.

## 6. International football before FIFA's tournament

The international tournaments held in Italy in 1970 and Mexico in 1971 preceded the first FIFA Women's World Cup in China in 1991. FIFA Museum includes the earlier events in its account of women's football. That sequence helps distinguish a competition's organiser from its significance to the people who played and watched it.

Calling an earlier competition unofficial in relation to FIFA can be accurate. Using that word to suggest the football was unreal would be misleading. A result can matter to participants without belonging to a later institution's championship record. Equally, enthusiasm for the pioneers does not justify relabelling their tournament as a FIFA event. Respect means preserving both parts of the account: acknowledge the achievement and identify the competition correctly. Precision strengthens recognition rather than diminishing it.

## 7. Whose work enters the record?

Historical collections naturally favour events that left photographs, reports or surviving objects. That can give a distorted impression if we forget how unevenly evidence survives. A famous team may have an extensive archive while a local side appears in only a few records. The difference in documentation does not prove a difference in the players' commitment or the meaning of their football.

When evidence is limited, the responsible answer may be that something is unknown. Inventing a score or a personal quotation makes a story smoother but less trustworthy. Asking what is missing can be more valuable. Who recorded the event? Which participants were named? What further evidence would resolve the uncertainty? These questions treat lesser-known players with respect because they protect their real lives from being replaced by convenient fiction.

## 8. When an open door is difficult to reach

Formal access is an essential gain, yet a player may still face cost, travel or unsuitable arrangements. These are not identical to the 1921 restriction. The comparison concerns the gap between an opportunity existing on paper and being usable in practice. Understanding that gap prevents us treating one administrative change as a complete solution for everyone.

Suppose a club advertises a girls' session, but the only available journey cannot arrive before it begins. The welcome is real, and the obstacle is real. Listening to families might reveal an adjustment worth exploring. It might also show that the club cannot solve every problem alone. Honest limits and practical discussion are more useful than blaming the player for failing to use an opportunity that she cannot reasonably reach.

## 9. Listening is part of access

Play Their Way describes child-first coaching through voice, choice and journey. Those ideas concern children's participation and development, rather than promising any particular selection outcome. They offer a way to examine what happens after a player comes through the gate. Does she have room to ask questions? Can her views shape the experience? Is development understood as more than immediate performance?

Listening does not require a coach to agree to every request. It requires taking a view seriously, explaining decisions and exploring workable options. A player might prefer a demonstration to a long verbal instruction. Another might need a private conversation before joining an unfamiliar activity. Responding thoughtfully helps turn a nominal place in a group into an experience in which someone can participate with confidence and understanding.

## 10. Keeping the gate open

The history of access is not a simple march in which every change automatically remains secure. Opportunities require maintenance: people, resources and decisions that continue to support them. That does not mean ignoring progress. It means understanding how progress is made durable. A pitch opened once needs arrangements that let the next group use it too.

The right to play asks us to notice who controls those arrangements and whose needs are heard. Remember the sequence running through this story: restrictions affected infrastructure, players and organisers sustained alternatives, institutions changed in stages, and practical access still required work. Your own contribution might be modest: welcoming a new teammate, respecting a question or helping someone find the right person to speak to. The historical lesson is larger than any individual gesture, but those gestures can help keep a hard-won opportunity usable.

# P04 — Beyond the Trophy

What we win. What we leave.

## 1. The result and the questions after it

A trophy gives a competition a clear ending. The final is finished, the winners are named and a photograph preserves the celebration. The history surrounding that photograph keeps moving. Players return to training or work, younger children see the images and organisers decide what to do next. A result can be settled while its wider significance remains open.

This card asks how to admire exceptional performance and still examine what follows. It does not require every team to transform society before its achievement counts. Winning a football competition is already a sporting accomplishment. The additional question is whether attention, knowledge or resources help create opportunities beyond the victorious squad. To answer fairly, we must distinguish documented results from possible influences and from changes that would need further evidence.

## 2. Arsenal's narrow European margin

Arsenal won the UEFA Women's Cup in 2007 against Umeå. Alex Scott scored in the away leg; a goalless return secured a 1–0 aggregate victory. The European title formed part of Arsenal's four major trophies in the 2006–07 season. The distinction between a match score and an aggregate score is essential to understanding how that success was secured.

A familiar celebration can make a triumph seem comfortable in retrospect. A one-goal margin across two matches reminds us that it was contested. The opponents had their own quality and ambition. Preserving uncertainty respects both sides. It also helps explain the collective nature of a result: the decisive goal needed the rest of the tie to be managed. A trophy photograph cannot show every intervention that protected that advantage.

## 3. What the score does not list

The Arsenal match report describes goalkeeper Emma Byrne and defensive work as important parts of the return leg. That evidence gives us a useful correction to a story told only through the goalscorer. Different actions contributed to one outcome. Naming Scott's goal accurately and noticing other contributions are complementary ways of understanding the match.

Think of the difference between a decisive moment and a sufficient explanation. A goal can decide the score without explaining everything that allowed a team to win. Preparation, substitutions, recovery runs and communication may matter too, though individual claims require appropriate evidence. This is a lesson for watching as well as reading. Look for the action away from the ball and ask what it enables, rather than treating the most replayed moment as the whole performance.

## 4. Japan's path was not unbeaten

Japan's 2011 World Cup campaign included a group-stage defeat by England. They then beat Germany after extra time in the quarter-final and Sweden in the semi-final before meeting the United States. The final ended 2–2 after extra time, with Japan winning the penalty shoot-out. Aya Miyama and Homare Sawa scored the equalising goals.

Knowing about the earlier defeat changes the story. The title was not evidence that the team never encountered a setback. Nor does the eventual victory prove that the defeat was necessary or beneficial. What we can say is that it did not end their tournament. This distinction prevents an easy mistake: using the happy ending to claim that every earlier difficulty must have happened for a helpful reason. The record supports persistence, not destiny.

## 5. A celebration during a difficult year

Japan's victory came after the earthquake and tsunami of March 2011. The JFA places the team's achievement within that national context. Football offered a shared moment of joy during a year of immense loss. Describing that meaning respectfully requires recognising the limits of the sporting event alongside its emotional importance.

A trophy does not rebuild a home, restore a life or give everyone the same experience of grief. It can still matter deeply. Holding these ideas together is more accurate than either dismissing the joy or presenting it as a cure. An individual supporter may remember a match as a treasured moment without claiming the disaster was overcome through football. The value of that memory does not depend on making a larger claim than the evidence can bear.

## 6. Success is not one measurement

Compare two questions. Who won the competition? What changed because of the attention around it? The first can often be answered from a result. The second requires different evidence: participation figures, access arrangements, investment or accounts from the people affected. A packed celebration cannot supply all those answers by itself.

This does not reduce the achievement. It protects it from being asked to prove too much. We can celebrate a winning team and still ask whether local players gained practical opportunities. We can also find valuable work by a team that did not win a trophy. Sporting success and contribution to a community are different measures. Sometimes they reinforce each other; sometimes one is more visible than the other. A thoughtful history makes room for both.

## 7. Institutions can preserve a legacy

A museum can keep a shirt, a ball or a trophy long after the players retire. FIFA Museum's recognition of Danish pioneers includes objects from the 1970 tournament in Italy. Preserving those materials helps visitors encounter a chapter that preceded FIFA's own Women's World Cup. The object can therefore connect a particular event with a wider history.

An object still needs interpretation. A trophy's label should identify the competition accurately. A jersey should not be assigned to the wrong year merely because it looks familiar. A photograph should not be altered into a false record of who was present. Display and storytelling have responsibilities because later audiences often meet an event first through those forms. The more impressive the presentation, the more care its underlying account deserves.

## 8. From attention to an ordinary session

Imagine a school receiving requests for girls' football after pupils watch a major final. The interest is an opening, but an opening becomes an opportunity only if someone can organise a usable session. Timing, supervision, equipment and an appropriate welcome all matter. This is a hypothetical example, not evidence that one particular trophy automatically caused one particular programme.

It shows why the word legacy should lead to practical questions. What was provided? Who could use it? Did it continue? How do we know? Those questions can reveal genuine gains and remaining gaps. They also prevent the burden falling entirely on the celebrated players. Organisers and institutions control many of the decisions needed to turn public enthusiasm into something a child can attend on an ordinary afternoon.

## 9. The opponent remains part of history

Celebrating winners need not turn opponents into anonymous obstacles. Umeå's place in Arsenal's final and the United States' place in Japan's final are part of what made those contests meaningful. A close match has tension because more than one outcome is possible. Removing the opponents' agency makes the winners' task seem easier than it was.

For a player, this offers a way to respect competition. You can want to win intensely while recognising that the other team is pursuing its own aims. After a defeat, learning may involve examining particular actions rather than deciding the whole team has failed as people. After a victory, learning still continues. Neither result gives a complete judgement on every performance or every individual's contribution. The scoreboard answers an important question, but it is not equipped to answer every question.

## 10. What remains after the cabinet closes

The title asks us to look beyond a trophy without looking past the football that earned it. Remember Arsenal's aggregate margin, Japan's recovery after a group defeat and the distinction between shared joy and material recovery. Each example resists an account in which the final photograph explains everything. Detailed history makes those achievements richer.

Your own legacy does not require a cup. It could include a teammate feeling able to try again because you responded well to her mistake, or a younger player learning from your example. These possibilities are not substitutes for organised support, and they cannot be measured by a medal count. They are ways of contributing to a game that continues after your match ends. Celebrate the result honestly, recognise the people behind it and ask what conditions will help the next players make their own memories.

# P05 — Her Game, Her Future

The next chapter belongs to her.

## 1. A collection becomes a responsibility

You have reached the final Prestige story. The cards behind it preserve different people, teams and events, but learning their names is only part of understanding them. A collection becomes more useful when it changes the questions you bring to football. Who has a chance to participate? Whose contribution is overlooked? What evidence supports the story being told?

This final chapter combines historical reflection with clearly identified imagined situations. The situations are not missing episodes from famous players' lives. They are opportunities to apply what the collection has taught. Their purpose is to practise judgement when several reasonable concerns exist together. Remembering a date is valuable; using knowledge carefully means recognising what a date can explain, what it cannot explain and when somebody else's voice needs to be heard.

## 2. The player is not a project

A young footballer may want to improve a skill, enjoy her team or explore a higher level. Those goals can change. An adult's ambition for her should not silently become the only acceptable destination. Play Their Way describes child-first coaching through voice, choice and journey: being heard, helping shape play and developing in an individual way.

Those ideas do not remove the coach's responsibility to teach. They ask how teaching relates to the person learning. A coach can provide a demanding activity while explaining its purpose and hearing how the player experiences it. A player can work towards a difficult goal without promising to keep that goal forever. Treating her as a person with developing interests protects the value of the game in her life as well as the quality of her learning.

## 3. An ambition chosen together

Imagine a winger who says she wants to attend a trial. Her coach notices that she avoids receiving the ball when closely marked. There are several possible explanations: uncertainty about the movement, discomfort with contact, a previous experience or something else. The coach should not decide which explanation is true merely from the visible behaviour.

A useful conversation could connect her goal with a manageable next step. She might practise showing for the ball with an adjusted level of pressure, then discuss what helped. The important connection is between her chosen ambition, appropriate support and a specific action. Sending her straight into the hardest possible task would not establish how committed she is. Challenge becomes useful when it offers a workable opportunity to learn, rather than serving as a public test of character.

## 4. Mistakes need information

Now imagine the same player loses possession in a match. A general judgement about her courage gives little guidance. A specific observation might be that her first touch brought the ball towards pressure when space was available elsewhere. A demonstration and another attempt could help her understand the difference. The example is hypothetical, but the distinction is practical.

Correction can be part of respectful coaching. Respect does not require pretending every action worked. It requires explaining the action without turning the mistake into a verdict on the person. The player should have room to ask what was meant and describe what she noticed. Learning becomes a shared investigation of the football problem. That approach leaves a clearer next step than praise or criticism detached from anything she can actually try.

## 5. Progress has more than one pace

Historical stories can make determination seem like constant forward movement. Real development includes pauses, changes of direction and periods when other parts of life need attention. A player may choose a different playing level or want a lighter commitment. That choice does not erase what she has learned or mean that earlier ambitions were dishonest.

Imagine a teenager balancing school demands with football. Before suggesting a solution, an adult needs to understand what she wants to keep and what feels difficult. The answer may concern timing, the environment or a wish to do something else. Listening does not guarantee a simple adjustment will solve everything. It does make the next decision better informed. A plan should serve the player's life, and should be open to review as that life changes.

## 6. Belonging requires a practical response

The Flying Stars example in the collection showed how equipment and transport can affect participation. That lesson can inform ordinary football without equating different circumstances. In an imagined local session, a player might struggle with a crowded explanation or an unfamiliar routine. A coach could ask privately what would help and offer a clear demonstration or predictable sequence.

The response should follow what is learned about that individual. It should not depend on a stereotype or on expecting her to explain personal information to the whole group. Adaptation is useful when it makes the activity understandable and achievable while preserving a worthwhile football challenge. It does not have to mean removing every difficulty. The question is which difficulty belongs to the learning task and which unnecessary obstacle is preventing her from engaging with it.

## 7. A teammate has influence too

Players do not control all the conditions around them. They can still influence how a session feels. Imagine a newcomer asking where to stand in an unfamiliar practice. An impatient answer can make asking again harder. A clear explanation and an invitation to join can help her begin. Neither action changes the whole structure of football, but both affect a real moment.

Support should also respect boundaries. A teammate does not need to become somebody's counsellor or promise to keep a worrying situation secret. If a player needs help beyond what a friend can offer, involving an appropriate trusted adult can be the useful next step. Belonging grows through small acts and responsible support together. Nobody should have to carry the entire responsibility for another person's wellbeing simply because they want to be kind.

## 8. Tell the past accurately

You may now know stories that friends have never encountered. Sharing them creates another responsibility. Distinguish the 1921 ground restriction from a criminal ban on every kick. Distinguish the FA's 1970 Council decision from the 1971 rule amendment. Distinguish tournaments before FIFA's Women's World Cup from the competition that began in 1991. These details are not obstacles to a good story; they are its foundation.

The same care applies to images. A symbolic illustration can communicate a theme without pretending to be a photograph of a particular event. A documented photograph needs an accurate description. When something is unknown, saying so keeps trust intact. The people in a collection deserve the same care you would want someone to take with your own name, achievements and experiences. Interesting history does not need invented certainty.

## 9. Choose what success means now

Consider three imagined players. One wants a professional career. One values playing with friends. One is deciding whether to return after a break. A useful football environment can take all three seriously, even though their immediate plans differ. Ambition is not measured only by choosing the most selective pathway. Commitment can be expressed through learning, reliability and care for a team at many levels.

For each player, a meaningful next step depends on what she wants and what support is available. The professional ambition deserves honest preparation without a guarantee. The friendship goal deserves worthwhile coaching without being dismissed. The returning player deserves room to settle without proving she never should have left. These examples connect the collection's concern for access with a future in which different forms of participation can remain valuable.

## 10. Pass the game forward

The collection began with people whose opportunities were shaped by work, institutions, conflict, resources and the choices of others. It ends with your own choices, while recognising that responsibility is shared. You cannot personally remove every barrier. You can ask a useful question, treat a teammate with respect and notice when a situation calls for help from someone with the right responsibility.

The final challenge is to keep learning without deciding that everyone must follow your route. Let the past widen your understanding rather than become a standard used to judge another player's difficulties. Remember courage, but also remember organisation and support. Celebrate achievement, but also protect the freedom to change direction. Her game and her future belong to the player herself. The most lasting contribution you make may be helping someone feel that there is still a place for her in football.
